Imagine working with an advanced AI system on a scientific problem you have spent years trying to understand. You present the problem, the machine examines enormous amounts of information and returns twenty possible explanations. You compare them with what you already know and choose the three that seem most promising. The AI follows those directions, and eventually one leads to an important discovery. You would have every reason to call that a successful interaction, but I would still want to know what happened to the other seventeen possibilities. What if one of them could have led somewhere even more significant, but appeared too unfamiliar, contradicted an accepted explanation or simply failed to interest the person making the decision? We may never know, because the research has already moved elsewhere.

This is what I am investigating next in THE FRONTIER series. The first paper, [*The Frontier May Exist in the Interaction*](https://teffpapers.com/papers/the-frontier-may-exist-in-the-interaction), asked where useful intelligence appears when humans, machines, tools and physical reality begin influencing one another. The second, [*When the Machine Notices First*](https://teffpapers.com/papers/when-the-machine-notices-first), examined [Anthropic's account of the ART research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-discovers-novel-enzyme-system), in which a Claude agent identified a genomic arrangement the team described as a previously uncharacterised system. One of the most revealing details was the [reported failure of ten reruns to rediscover the array](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/22573675ada52a8ca8a97a1a4b4326b2f208a071.pdf), even though agents encountered related genomic regions. The path through the problem mattered. Now I want to examine how a possibility is selected for further investigation, and what makes it worth pursuing while another is left behind. If the frontier may exist in the interaction, we also need to understand how that interaction chooses its direction.

We already make these choices whenever we do research. Previous knowledge helps us separate promising evidence from noise, experience helps us recognise weak explanations and available time or laboratory resources determine what can be tested. None of that is inherently a problem. Without selection, even the most capable research system could spend its time following endless possibilities that lead nowhere. But a possibility can also be dropped before anyone has enough evidence to establish whether it mattered and increasingly capable AI makes this tension harder to ignore because generating candidate explanations is becoming easier than investigating all of them. AI can produce more possibilities while decisions throughout the interaction determine which receive further attention.

Physicist Matthew Schwartz encountered a version of this while working with Claude and a scientific computing toolkit called BootLoops. Claude could recognise that mathematical methods used in one discipline might solve difficult problems in another, including ecology and population genetics. Yet a technically impressive answer was not always an important scientific result. In one ecology project, Claude helped solve a calculation that had been difficult to carry out at scale and the team used it to show that the mix of tree species in a well-studied forest changed faster than a particular neutral theory predicted. When Schwartz brought the result to ecologist James O'Dwyer, the response was revealing: the computation was impressive, but ecologists already had good reasons to doubt the theory's fit with real forests. O'Dwyer suggested a more useful direction—subtract the neutral prediction and investigate what remained. Working with Claude, they developed a model intended to capture the biological differences the original comparison had not explained. Schwartz describes this work in [his account of Claude-shaped science](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-shaped-science) as part of an ongoing research programme.

What changed that investigation was not simply a better calculation. The specialist understood how the result related to what ecologists already knew, challenged its scientific importance and redirected the work toward a more useful question. But this also brings us to the other side of the problem. The same experience that allows someone to recognise a weak research direction can influence how they respond to an unfamiliar one. Existing knowledge helps us avoid repeating old work, yet it can also shape what we expect a worthwhile discovery to look like. That does not make expertise unreliable; it makes the interaction between expertise and machine exploration worth examining. How can the interaction benefit from what the expert knows without treating either participant's expectations as sufficient reason to dismiss an unfamiliar explanation?

Anthropic's [scorpion-neurotoxin protein-design experiment](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-scorpion-toxin-binder-design) makes the selection problem more visible. In a campaign exploring possible starting points for future antivenom research, Claude generated 318,484 protein designs. The model selected 180 candidates for laboratory testing, and the research team added another 31 with its help. Two external laboratories tested all 211 designs, of which seventeen met the researchers' criteria for binding to specific toxins. The team has not established whether these binders neutralise scorpion venom, so this is evidence of binding, not a working antivenom. Even with that limitation, the experiment shows how many decisions separate a proposed design from experimental evidence that it binds to its target.

Look at the decisions within that sequence. Humans helped establish the medical problem and the target molecules. Claude explored possible designs and chose some of the quality thresholds used during its campaign. Human follow-up instructions changed the work, the model selected candidates, the research team expanded the shortlist, and laboratory measurements determined which designs met the researchers' binding criteria. Each decision affected what could be examined next, and the laboratory results could give the participants reasons to reconsider earlier choices. We have no basis for claiming that the hundreds of thousands of untested designs contained a better treatment. Most may have been unsuitable. The point is that the value of any design would still have to be established through selection and testing, and neither the human nor the machine controlled every part of that process.

It would be tempting to conclude that the answer is simply better human judgement, but a study published in *Scientometrics* gives us a reason to be more careful. Diogo Machado examined 139 proposals submitted to the Austrian Science Fund in 2021 across four scientific fields, using GPT-4o to simulate funding selections through comparisons between proposals. Those selections matched actual funding decisions in 60% of cases. Proposals rated as more novel by human reviewers were more likely to be funded by humans but not selected by the model. [Machado's study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11192-026-05792-x) therefore found a relationship between human-assessed novelty and disagreement, not evidence that rejected proposals would have produced better discoveries. Its scope also matters: one funder, one submission year and one model configuration, with the model comparing proposal texts rather than being given the human reviewers' full evaluation framework. It does not establish how AI evaluators generally respond to unfamiliar science.

Still, consider what that result means for our investigation. We worry that a scientist may overlook an unfamiliar idea because of personal experience or established knowledge, yet this model-based selection process also favoured different proposals when human-assessed novelty was high. The study does not establish why, and agreement with a funding decision is not a measure of scientific truth. But it challenges a comfortable assumption: moving a decision from a person to an AI does not necessarily remove the influences that narrow a search. It may introduce different ones. If the system helps generate possibilities and then participates in ranking which deserve resources, its way of assessing value becomes part of the scientific path being taken.

These three cases bring us back to the same question from different directions. BootLoops shows how expert knowledge can turn technically correct work toward a more meaningful question. The scorpion experiment shows how machine-generated possibilities are selected through human instructions, model decisions and physical tests. The proposal study shows that a model-based selection process can disagree systematically with human decisions. Across all three, the question is not whether humans or AI should have the final say. It is how decisions pass between participants, what information each decision uses and whether the interaction leaves room to challenge a choice that initially seemed reasonable. A participant who proposes a direction in one round may be evaluating or revising it in the next.

There is another complication. A machine that works with one scientist for years may become exceptionally good at understanding that person's interests, methods and standards of evidence. This could make the collaboration more productive but it could also affect what the system chooses to present. If it learns that certain lines of inquiry rarely receive attention, will it stop bringing them forward? Or could that same understanding allow it to identify when the scientist is overlooking something and explain why an unusual possibility deserves another look? Both outcomes are plausible but neither has been demonstrated by the cases discussed here. We should not assume either will follow simply because a model becomes more capable or more personalised. What the model remembers, which alternatives it preserves, how it explains its ranking and whether its selections can be questioned may all influence the result.

I think this is something we can begin investigating through our own interactions as well. Give the same unfamiliar problem to several AI systems, compare what they propose and what they dismiss, and then change the information or preferences used to guide their selections while keeping the problem and available resources as comparable as possible. If a human expert joins halfway through, does the system find a better direction, or does it merely become more likely to favour that expert's existing interests? If a rejected possibility is preserved and revisited after new evidence arrives, does it become more useful? Repeated trials and independent checks would be needed before making strong claims, especially because a discarded possibility is not a missed discovery just because it sounds intriguing. A change in rankings would not by itself demonstrate better science but even a limited experiment could help us see how different arrangements change what gets investigated.

Existing work also shows why we should examine decisions at more than one stage. In [research with PerturbAI and Scripps Research](https://openai.com/index/scaling-ai-research-for-complex-biological-data/), OpenAI used distributed agents to analyse existing experimental data from millions of mouse brain cells and connect the results with scientific literature. One lead linked disruption of the mouse gene Dctn1 with changes in cholesterol-related gene expression. Human experts considered the resulting hypothesis worth pursuing, but the account presents it as a direction for follow-up experiments, not an experimentally established biological mechanism. In Brice Ménard's [ultraviolet sky-mapping project](https://www.anthropic.com/research/the-missing-map-of-the-sky), the sequence was different: he noticed an error that had survived two rounds of agent review, and the agents then traced and corrected it. Neither example gives humans or machines one permanent responsibility. An observation can come from either, its importance can be reassessed, and new evidence can change what happens next.

That returns us to the researcher who selected three explanations from twenty. The successful discovery deserves recognition but it cannot tell us what the other seventeen would have produced. Some may never have been worth pursuing; others may become relevant under different evidence, tools or circumstances. We cannot investigate every path and it would be unreasonable to measure a successful interaction against discoveries no one can demonstrate were possible. Yet we can ask whether its decisions were open to challenge, whether unusual possibilities were preserved rather than lost and whether new evidence could change the direction chosen earlier. **The frontier may exist in the interaction, but what becomes discoverable may also depend on how that interaction decides what matters.** Understanding those decisions is as important to the next stage of this research as understanding the capabilities of the participants making them.
