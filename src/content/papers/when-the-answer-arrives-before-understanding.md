Think about a mathematician working on a problem that has been open for decades. Several promising approaches have failed and they have begun to suspect that the problem needs a method no one in the field has tried. Then an AI system gives them a complete argument, in this case not a suggestion about where to look but a direct proof that appears to settle the question. They will be able to read it but still need to check whether it establishes what it claims, why the construction works and what it changes about the problem they thought they understood. They were expecting to develop an understanding that would lead to an answer, but now they have an answer from which they have to work back towards an understanding.

That reversal takes THE FRONTIER further into the interaction I began examining in [The Frontier May Exist in the Interaction](https://teffpapers.com/papers/the-frontier-may-exist-in-the-interaction). In [When the Machine Notices First](https://teffpapers.com/papers/when-the-machine-notices-first), the machine identified something the researchers had not recognised and gave them a reason to change direction, while [What Decides What Matters?](https://teffpapers.com/papers/what-decides-what-matters) followed the decisions that determine which possibilities receive further investigation. Now the machine has gone beyond supplying an observation or a direction. It has produced an argument the researchers did not reach themselves which is different from producing one they cannot understand. Mathematicians have always had to make sense of one another’s proofs but I am interested in seeing what happens when the work of finding a proof and the work of understanding it begin to move at different speeds.

In May 2026, [OpenAI reported that one of its models had disproved Erdős’s unit-distance conjecture](https://openai.com/index/model-disproves-discrete-geometry-conjecture/), which concerned how many pairs of points in a plane could be exactly one unit apart as more points were added. The model found an unexpected arrangement using ideas from number theory and showed that the number of such pairs could grow faster than the conjecture allowed, without determining the maximum. Noga Alon, Tim Gowers and their colleagues checked the argument and [wrote a shorter account](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20695) that traced the ideas it relied on, while [Will Sawin](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20579) took the construction further and put a concrete lower bound on how fast that number could grow. The model had answered the question of whether the conjecture was true, but its proof also gave mathematicians a method to examine, explain and improve. That part of the discovery could begin because they had the answer.

The geometry case shows what mathematicians can do once they have an argument worth studying but an argument can be correct without being easy to work with. In [First Proof’s October evaluation](https://hexagonmath.org/2610.00244v1), five AI systems tackled ten research-level problems whose human solutions had not been made public. The strongest system, ProofCouncil, solved nine and every problem was solved by at least one of the systems. Note that these were not ten new discoveries and the scores tell us about the systems on these problems, not about their abilities across other fields. What makes the test useful here is that [the reviewers judged correctness, clarity and attribution separately](https://1stproof.org/third-batch-press-release.html), and even the strongest system did not do equally well on all three. Sometimes even with a sound proof, a mathematician might have to reconstruct why a step was taken or trace an idea back to the work it relied on.

That work takes on a different scale when the results concern open problems and there is no known solution to compare them with. On 26 October, [OpenAI released written arguments and Lean formalisations for two of three announced results](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/), while the group reviewing the proposed physics results initially held back the material to work through it. For the claimed refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis, [the group convened to examine it](https://ethdiscussion.blog/2026/10/26/eth-refutation-examination-and-discussion/) asked for the materials to be made public and made clear that its involvement was not an endorsement. [Ryan O’Donnell’s subsequent explanation of part of the argument](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~odonnell/some_eth_false_exposition.pdf) shows what that arrangement makes possible: researchers can begin to make a difficult result easier to examine without treating the whole claim as settled.

The physics review shows how much of that work can happen before the proof is fully understood. On 10 November, the Yang–Mills group released the submitted Lean code with [its first assessment](https://github.com/yang-mills-group/YM/blob/main/docs/assessment-1.pdf). The reviewers checked what the mathematical statements meant as well as whether the code passed, and found that the submission establishes a construction of four-dimensional pure Yang–Mills theory under the conditions they examined. It did not establish a mass gap or settle the full Millennium Prize problem, and the assessment did not extend to the broader claims about quantum chromodynamics or the Standard Model. Interestingly the reviewers could say what the formal submission established while acknowledging they did not yet fully understand the proof. They used AI to help them work through it so the interaction that produced the result continued in a different form as they tried to understand it.

The scope of that assessment is important because Lean can check a proof against the statement written in its formal language, but the check alone cannot tell us whether that statement captures the intended mathematical claim or whether the formal proof follows the written argument. An [October preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08144) on [OpenAI’s September Navier–Stokes work](https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/) reported differences between parts of the written proof and their formal counterparts. While it does not show that the written conclusion is false, it shows why we still need to know what the tool checked before deciding how much we rely on it.

Even once a result has been checked, there is a decision about how much effort to spend understanding it. This is where the problem from What Decides What Matters? changes for me. The researcher in that paper had to choose which possible explanations to investigate without knowing what the others would yield. Now the choice may be which finished arguments to examine, and the value of an unfamiliar result may not be apparent until someone sees what its method can do. That does not make every unread proof a missed discovery but it means that a correct result can go unused because recognising its value takes time and the people best placed to do that work cannot examine every result. If machines begin to produce results faster than researchers can give them that attention, the difficulty is no longer only choosing which questions to pursue but choosing which answers to learn from.

How much we need to understand depends on what we intend to do. A mathematician can use a verified theorem without knowing why its author took every step while a researcher deciding whether a predictive model can be used under different conditions needs to know more than how well it performed in its original test. The mathematician in the opening may be able to move on with a new theorem but if they take the time to study the proof, they may find a technique more useful than the result it first established. Understanding the answer does more than explain how it was reached because it can also change which problem we are able to ask next.

I began THE FRONTIER asking whether some discoveries are better understood by examining the interaction than by looking at the human or the machine alone. **The frontier may exist in the interaction**, but these cases do not settle where the intelligence belongs. They give me a reason to follow the work beyond the moment an answer appears, because the ability to produce results may advance ahead of our understanding without making that understanding any less necessary. What we choose to check, what we can explain to someone else and what we recognise as worth pursuing can all change what we ask the machine to do next, so an answer that arrives early may give the interaction a new direction rather than bring it to an end.
